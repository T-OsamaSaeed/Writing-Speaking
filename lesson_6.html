Lesson 6

Formal & Informal Writing, Writing Styles, and Natural English

Lesson Goals

Distinguish formal, neutral, and informal

Choose a suitable tone for the situation

Adapt messages for different audiences

Recognize the main writing styles

Use appropriate greetings and closings

Avoid overly direct or emotional language

Use contractions and abbreviations properly

Use collocations & phrasal verbs

Part One: Writing Skills

1. Audience, Purpose, and Tone

Before writing, consider three things. These elements affect vocabulary, sentence structure, greetings, requests, politeness, contractions, and closings.

1. Audience

Who will read it?

  • - Friend or family member
  • - Colleague or manager
  • - Teacher or university
  • - Customer or company
  • - General public

2. Purpose

Why are you writing?

  • - Giving/asking for info
  • - Making a request
  • - Explaining a problem
  • - Apologizing/Complaining
  • - Persuading/Describing

3. Tone

How should it sound?

  • - Formal, Neutral, Informal
  • - Friendly, Polite, Serious
  • - Professional, Objective
  • - Persuasive, Personal

You should not write to a university in the same style that you use when messaging a friend.

2. Formal, Neutral, and Informal Writing

English is not divided into only formal and informal language. Neutral language is also very common.

2.1 Formal Writing

Used in: Academic writing, reports, university communication, official apps, complaints, business.

Uses: Complete forms, polite language, precise vocabulary, clear organization, fewer personal expressions, no slang, few/no contractions.

"I am writing to request further information regarding the programme."

"I would appreciate it if you could confirm the application deadline."

"Unfortunately, I will be unable to attend the meeting."

2.2 Neutral Writing

Polite and clear without sounding extremely formal. Often the safest choice.

Used in: Workplace emails, messages to colleagues, everyday professional situations, customer service.

"Could you please send me the updated document?"

"I wanted to ask whether the meeting time has changed."

"Thank you for letting me know."

2.3 Informal Writing

Used with: Friends, close colleagues, family, social media.

Uses: Contractions, shorter sentences, everyday vocabulary, personal comments, emojis, abbreviations.

"Hi! How are you?"

"I just wanted to ask if you are free tomorrow."

"Sorry I could not reply earlier. Thanks for your help!"

4. Weak and Strong Tone Comparison

Situation 1: You need information from a university

Too Direct

"Send me the course information."

Better Neutral Version

"Could you please send me the course information?"

Strong Formal Version

"I would appreciate it if you could provide further information about the course."

Situation 2: A payment is missing

Too Direct (Weak)

"I paid the money. Why did you not receive it? Check it and tell me."

Problems: Tone sounds demanding, sentences are too direct, payment not identified clearly, expected action is expressed impolitely.

Strong / Polite

"I am writing regarding the payment I submitted on 15 July. I have attached the payment confirmation for reference. Could you please check whether the payment has been received and let me know if any additional documentation is required?"

5. Contractions

Contractions combine two words. Examples: I am → I'm, you are → you're, do not → don't, cannot → can't, I have → I've, we will → we'll.

Informal and Neutral Writing

Contractions are common and natural.

"I'm sorry I couldn't reply earlier."

"We'll send the information tomorrow."

Formal Writing

Complete forms are often preferred.

"I am writing to confirm the appointment."

"We will provide the final decision next week."

Important note: Contractions are not always incorrect in professional English. Modern workplace emails often use them. However, complete forms create a more formal tone.

6. Informal Abbreviations and Slang

Informal messages may contain abbreviations such as: ASAP, FYI, BTW, LOL, IDK, thanks, info. These should be used carefully.

Comparison of Use

Informal "BTW, are you free tomorrow?"
Professional "By the way, are you available tomorrow?"
Formal "I would also like to ask whether you are available tomorrow."

Avoid slang and texting abbreviations in:

  • Essays
  • Reports
  • University emails
  • Applications
  • Formal complaints
  • Professional documents

7. Polite Requests

Direct language may sound rude even when the writer does not intend to be rude.

Direct Command

"Send me the document."

Polite Request

"Could you please send me the document?"

More Formal Request

"I would appreciate it if you could send me the document."

Common Request Structures

Can you...? Casual or neutral: Can you check this?
Could you...? More polite: Could you check this?
Would you mind...? Polite (use Gerund): Would you mind checking this?

Would you mind to check this?

I would appreciate it if... Formal: I would appreciate it if you could review the attached document.
I would be grateful if... Very polite and formal: I would be grateful if you could confirm the deadline.

8. Softening Language

Softening language makes requests, criticism, and disagreement more polite.

Useful Softeners Include:

perhaps, possibly, a little, slightly, may, might, could, it seems, I am afraid, unfortunately

Direct

"Your report is unclear."

Softer

"Some parts of the report may need further clarification."

Direct

"You made a mistake."

Softer

"There appears to be a small error in the document."

Direct

"This plan will not work."

Softer

"This plan may be difficult to implement in its current form."

Important principle: Soft language should remain clear. Politeness should not make the meaning impossible to understand.

10. Personal and Impersonal Language

Formal writing may use impersonal structures, which avoid starting sentences with "I" or "We".

Personal

"I think the results show that students need more support."

More Objective

"The results suggest that students may require additional support."

Personal

"We can see that attendance decreased."

Impersonal

"It can be seen that attendance decreased."

Useful Impersonal Structures

  • - It appears that...
  • - It seems that...
  • - It can be argued that...
  • - It is important to...
  • - It is recommended that...
  • - The results indicate that...
  • - The evidence suggests that...

Impersonal language is useful in: Reports, Academic writing, Formal analysis, Recommendations.

11. Objective and Subjective Language

Subjective Language

Shows personal feelings, opinions, or judgements.

"I think the service was terrible."

"The programme was amazing."

Objective Language

Focuses on observable facts or evidence.

"Several customers reported delays in the service."

"The programme received positive feedback from most participants."

Formal reports usually require more objective language.

14. Writing Styles

Writing style refers to the way language is used according to the writer’s purpose. The main styles in this lesson are Descriptive, Narrative, Informative (expository), Persuasive, and Reflective.

14.1 Descriptive Writing

Helps the reader imagine a person, place, object, atmosphere, or experience. Uses specific adjectives, sensory details, spatial order, and strong verbs.

Weak"The room was nice and big."
Strong"The room was spacious and bright, with large windows overlooking the garden."

14.2 Narrative Writing

Tells an event or story. Uses past tenses, time expressions, characters, events, problems, results, reflections.

"Last year, I had to give a presentation in front of a large audience. Although I felt nervous at first, I gradually became more confident..."

14.3 Informative Writing

Explains a topic clearly. Gives information, defines concepts, presents facts objectively.

"Time management is the process of organizing tasks according to their importance and deadlines. It can help individuals reduce stress..."

14.4 Persuasive Writing

Tries to convince the reader. Includes a clear opinion, strong reasons, explanations, and evidence.

"Schools should provide regular reading time because reading supports vocabulary... Even fifteen minutes of daily reading can help."

14.5 Reflective Writing

Explores a personal experience, feelings, and changes in understanding. Explains what an experience meant.

Weak: "I gave a presentation. I was nervous. It ended well."
Strong: "Giving the presentation showed me that nervousness does not always prevent good performance..."

15. Comparing Writing Styles

Topic: Learning from Mistakes

The topic may remain the same, but the purpose and style change.

Descriptive

His face became red, and his hands began to shake when he noticed the error.

Narrative

I made an important mistake during the project, but I corrected it before the deadline.

Informative

Mistakes are a natural part of the learning process because they show which areas need improvement.

Persuasive

Students should not be punished for every mistake because fear can reduce participation and confidence.

Reflective

The mistake initially embarrassed me, but it later helped me understand the importance of checking my work carefully.

16. Maintaining a Consistent Style

A text should not suddenly change from formal to very informal language.

Inconsistent Style

"The survey results indicate that most participants were satisfied. Anyway, the programme was super cool and everyone loved it!"

Problem: The first sentence is formal and objective. The second is highly informal and emotional.
Consistent Formal Version

"The survey results indicate that most participants were satisfied. Overall, the programme received highly positive feedback."

Why it is better: Both sentences maintain an objective, professional tone suitable for a report.

17. Weak and Strong Formal Writing Comparison

Weak Formal Email
Hi,

I need the information now because I have to apply. Send it quickly.

Thanks.
  • The opening is too casual for a formal recipient.
  • The request sounds demanding.
  • The purpose lacks detail.
  • "Now" and "quickly" sound impatient.
  • The closing is too abrupt.
Strong Formal Email
Dear Admissions Team,

I am preparing my application and would like to request further information about the required documents. I would appreciate it if you could send the relevant details at your earliest convenience.

Thank you for your assistance.

Kind regards,
Polite, clear, uses appropriate structures, and maintains a professional tone.

18. Weak and Strong Informal Writing

Weak Informal Email (To a friend)
Dear my friend,
I am writing to inform you that I cannot attend the dinner.

The language is too formal for a friend.

Natural Informal Version
Hi!
I’m sorry, but I can’t come to dinner tonight. Something unexpected has come up. I hope we can meet another day.

Natural, uses contractions, friendly tone.

19. Common Formality Problems

Avoid:

  • Using slang in formal writing
  • Using excessively complex vocabulary
  • Mixing contractions and highly formal expressions without purpose
  • Giving commands instead of requests
  • Using emotional language in reports
  • Writing long sentences that are difficult to understand
  • Using several exclamation marks
  • Beginning every formal sentence with I
  • Ending without a clear request or conclusion
  • Being so polite that the main message becomes unclear
Part Two: Speaking Skills

20. Natural English

Natural English does NOT mean:

  • - Using difficult words
  • - Using slang constantly
  • - Copying a particular accent
  • - Using idioms in every sentence
  • - Speaking without grammar

Natural English means:

  • - Using common word combinations (collocations)
  • - Choosing expressions suitable for the situation
  • - Speaking in familiar chunks
  • - Using clear rhythm and sentence patterns
  • - Avoiding direct translation
  • - Communicating ideas smoothly

24. Separable & Inseparable

Some phrasal verbs can be separated.

Separable (Turn down)

She turned down the offer.

She turned the offer down.

Rule for Pronouns: If the object is a pronoun, it MUST go in the middle.
Correct: She turned it down. Incorrect: She turned down it.
Inseparable (Look after)

She looks after the child.

She looks the child after.

Important: Position and meaning should be learned through complete examples.

26. Idioms

An idiom is an expression whose meaning is not always clear from the individual words.

Break the ice

To make people feel more comfortable at the beginning of a social situation.

"The teacher used a simple activity to break the ice."

Be on the same page

To share the same understanding.

"Before starting, we need to make sure everyone is on the same page."

Get the hang of something

To begin understanding how to do something.

"It was difficult at first, but I eventually got the hang of it."

Learn the hard way

To learn through a difficult or negative experience.

"I learned the hard way that I should always save a copy of my work."

In the long run

Over a long period.

"Regular practice is more effective in the long run."

Once in a while

Occasionally.

"I eat at restaurants once in a while."

A piece of cake / Under the weather

Very easy. / Feeling slightly ill.

"The first activity was a piece of cake." / "I am feeling a little under the weather today."

Hit the nail on the head

Describe the exact cause or truth.

"You hit the nail on the head when you said the main problem was communication."

27. Using Idioms Naturally

Idioms should:

  • Match the context
  • Be commonly understood
  • Fit the level of formality
  • Be used occasionally
Unnatural (Overused)

"The report was a piece of cake, and I hit the nail on the head, so we were on the same page in the long run."

Too many idioms make the sentence unnatural.

Natural

"The first task was a piece of cake, but the second one was much more challenging."

Important Rule

Idioms can make language more natural, but overusing them makes communication sound forced.

Avoid informal idioms in:

  • Academic essays
  • Formal reports
  • Official complaints
  • Legal/highly professional writing

28. Everyday Chunks

Chunks are groups of words commonly used together. They help you speak more smoothly.

Giving an Opinion

  • - In my opinion...
  • - From my point of view...
  • - The way I see it...
  • - Personally, I think...
  • - I would say that...

Giving Yourself Time

  • - Let me think for a moment.
  • - That is an interesting question.
  • - It is difficult to say.
  • - I have never thought about that before.
  • - How can I put this?

Clarifying

  • - What I mean is...
  • - In other words...
  • - Let me explain.
  • - More specifically...
  • - To put it differently...

Correcting Yourself

  • - Sorry, what I meant was...
  • - Let me rephrase that.
  • - I should say...
  • - More accurately...
  • - Actually...

Showing Agreement

  • - That makes sense.
  • - I completely agree.
  • - That is a good point.
  • - I feel the same way.
  • - Exactly.

Showing Polite Disagreement

  • - I am not sure I agree.
  • - I see it differently.
  • - That may be true, but...
  • - I understand your point; however...
  • - Another way to look at it is...

29. Formal and Informal Speaking

The same message can be expressed differently depending on the situation and who you are speaking to.

Asking for Help

Informal "Can you help me with this?"
Neutral "Could you help me with this?"
Formal "I would appreciate your assistance with this matter."

Saying You Did Not Understand

Informal "Sorry, I didn’t get that."
Neutral "Sorry, could you explain that again?"
Formal "I am afraid I did not fully understand. Could you please clarify?"

Disagreeing

Informal "I don’t think so."
Neutral "I am not sure I agree."
Formal "I understand the point; however, I would take a different view."

31. How to Sound More Natural

To sound more natural:

Learn collocations

Learn complete chunks

Notice how words are used in context

Use simple structures accurately

Listen to natural speech

Repeat useful phrases

Avoid translating word for word

Record yourself

Reuse corrected expressions

Choose language appropriate to situation

Do not try to sound natural by using slang you do not fully understand.

33. Common Problems to Avoid

In Writing Avoid:

  • Mixing formal and informal language
  • Using commands instead of polite requests
  • Using slang in academic or professional texts
  • Using overly formal words unnecessarily
  • Using emotional language in objective reports
  • Using contractions inconsistently
  • Writing without considering the audience
  • Using a friendly closing in a serious complaint
  • Adding several exclamation marks
  • Being so indirect that the purpose is unclear

In Speaking Avoid:

  • Using phrasal verbs without understanding them
  • Translating expressions word for word
  • Using too many idioms
  • Using slang in formal situations
  • Speaking too formally with close friends
  • Memorizing isolated words without collocations
  • Repeating basic words (good, bad, nice)
  • Using advanced vocabulary incorrectly
  • Changing tone suddenly
  • Copying expressions without understanding their meaning

34. Writing Self-Editing Checklist

Audience and Purpose

  • Who will read this?
  • Why am I writing?
  • What response do I expect?
  • Is the tone appropriate?

Formality

  • Is the language formal, neutral, or informal?
  • Did I maintain the same level throughout?
  • Are contractions appropriate?
  • Did I avoid slang and texting abbreviations?

Requests & Clarity

  • Is the request polite?
  • Is the expected action clear?
  • Did I avoid sounding demanding?
  • Is the purpose clear from the beginning?
  • Can the reader easily understand what I need?

Vocabulary & Style

  • Is the vocabulary precise?
  • Did I avoid unnecessarily difficult words?
  • Are the collocations natural?
  • Did I translate any expressions directly?
  • Does the language match the purpose?

35. Speaking Self-Assessment Checklist

After speaking, check:

  • Did I use the correct level of formality?
  • Did I choose natural expressions?
  • Did I use collocations correctly?
  • Did I use phrasal verbs appropriately?
  • Did I avoid too many idioms?
  • Did I translate any phrases directly?
  • Did I use complete chunks?
  • Did I speak clearly and simply?
  • Did I sound natural rather than memorized?
  • Did my tone suit the situation?
  • Did I correct myself naturally?
  • Did I remain polite?
