Part One: Writing Skills

1. What Is a Clause?

A clause is a group of words containing a subject and a verb.

Example: The student improved.

  • Subject: the student
  • Verb: improved

There are two main types of clauses:

1.1 Independent Clauses

Expresses a complete idea. It can stand alone as a sentence.

  • The lesson started.
  • She completed the assignment.
  • They are studying English.

Each contains a subject, a verb, and a complete meaning.

1.2 Dependent Clauses

Contains a subject and a verb, but does not express a complete idea by itself.

Begins with words like: because, although, when, if, while, since, unless, who, which, that.

Incomplete Because she wanted to improve. (The reader expects more info) Complete She practised every day because she wanted to improve.

Independent and Dependent Clause Comparison

Independent clauseShe attended the course.This is complete.
Dependent clauseAlthough she attended the courseThis is incomplete.
Complete sentenceAlthough she attended the course, she did not complete the homework.
Important rule: A dependent clause cannot normally stand alone as a complete sentence.

2. The Four Main Sentence Structures

English sentences can be classified according to the number and type of clauses they contain. The four main structures are: 1. Simple, 2. Compound, 3. Complex, 4. Compound-complex.

2.1 Simple Sentences

A simple sentence contains one independent clause.

Basic & Expanded

Basic: She studies.

Expanded: She studies English at home every evening.

The second sentence is longer, but it is still a simple sentence because it contains only one independent clause.

Multiple Subjects or Verbs

More than one subject: Osama and Ahmed work together.

More than one verb: She opened the book and started reading.

Both examples still contain only one independent clause.

Weak Use of Simple Sentences

"I woke up. I had breakfast. I left home. I went to work. I started teaching."

These sentences are grammatically correct, but the writing sounds repetitive and mechanical.

Improved Version

"I woke up early, had breakfast, and left home before eight. After arriving at work, I started preparing for my first lesson."

The improved version combines related information and creates a smoother flow.

2.2 Compound Sentences

A compound sentence contains: Two independent clauses and a coordinating conjunction or suitable punctuation.

Independent clause + conjunction + independent clause

"I wanted to attend the lesson, but I was feeling unwell."
Both parts can stand alone: "I wanted to attend the lesson." / "I was feeling unwell."

Connecting Clauses

FANBOYS

  • For — reason
  • And — addition
  • Nor — negative alternative
  • But — contrast
  • Or — choice
  • Yet — unexpected contrast
  • So — result
  • She studied grammar, and she completed the exercises.
  • The task was difficult, but she continued.
  • He was nervous, yet he spoke clearly.
  • I was tired, so I went home.

Comma Rule

Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction when it joins two independent clauses.

Correct: I wanted to call you, but I was busy.

No comma needed: I bought pens and notebooks. (Joins two nouns, not clauses)

With a Semicolon (;)

The lesson was challenging; the students remained focused.

With Conjunctive Adverbs

however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, consequently, otherwise, furthermore, meanwhile

The task was difficult; however, she completed it.

The task was difficult. However, she completed it.

The task was difficult, however she completed it.

A comma alone cannot normally join two independent clauses with however.

2.3 Complex Sentences

Contains one independent clause and at least one dependent clause.

"I practise every day because I want to improve."

Independent: I practise every day.
Dependent: because I want to improve

Comma Rules

Dependent first = Comma.
Although the task was difficult, she completed it.

Dependent second = No comma.
She completed the task although it was difficult.

Common Dependent Clauses

  • Reason (because, since, as): She practised because she wanted to improve.
  • Contrast (although, even though, while): Although she was tired, she continued.
  • Time (when, while, before, until): I will contact you when I receive it.
  • Condition (if, unless, provided that): You will improve if you practise regularly.
  • Purpose (so that, in order that): She recorded herself so that she could review.

2.4 Compound-Complex

Contains at least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.

"Although I was tired, I completed the assignment, and I submitted it before the deadline."

  • Dependent: Although I was tired
  • Independent 1: I completed the assignment
  • Independent 2: I submitted it before the deadline

"When the meeting ended, Sarah returned to her office, but her manager stayed in the conference room."

Contains: One dependent clause, Two independent clauses.

Important note: Compound-complex sentences can improve variety, but they should not be used only to make writing look advanced. Clarity is more important than length.

3. Comparing the Four Sentence Structures

TypeStructureExample
SimpleOne independent clauseShe studies English every day.
CompoundTwo independent clausesShe studies English, and she practises speaking.
ComplexIndependent + dependent clauseShe studies English because she wants to travel.
Compound-complexTwo independent + dependent clauseAlthough she is busy, she studies English, and she practises speaking.

4. Sentence Types According to Purpose

4.1 Declarative Sentences

Gives information or states an opinion. Ends with a full stop.

She works at a school. / I believe online education can be effective.

4.2 Interrogative Sentences

Asks a question. Ends with a question mark.

Do you enjoy reading? / Where do you work?

4.3 Imperative Sentences

Gives instruction, request, advice, or command. (Subject 'you' is understood).

Open the book. / Please take a seat. / Stop talking.

4.4 Exclamatory Sentences

Expresses strong emotion.

What a beautiful place! / How quickly time passes!

Note: Too many exclamation marks can make writing look emotional or unprofessional.

5. Combining Ideas

Strong writing does not contain only long sentences or only short sentences. It combines ideas according to their relationship.

5.1 Addition

and, also, in addition, moreover, furthermore

Basic: She studies English. She practises speaking.

Combined: She studies English, and she practises speaking.

Formal: She studies English regularly. In addition, she practises speaking through voice recordings.

5.2 Contrast

but, yet, although, even though, however, nevertheless, whereas

Basic: The course was difficult. It was useful.

Combined: The course was difficult, but it was useful.

Complex: Although the course was difficult, it was useful.

Formal: The course was difficult; however, it was useful.

5.3 Reason & 5.4 Result

Reason: because, since, as, for | Result: so, therefore, as a result, consequently

Reason: She practises every day because she wants to improve. (Since she wants to improve, she practises every day.)

Result: She wanted to improve, so she practised every day. (She practised every day; therefore, her confidence increased.)

5.5 Condition & 5.6 Time

Cond: if, unless, provided that, as long as | Time: when, while, before, after, until, as soon as

Cond: You will improve if you practise regularly. / You will not improve unless you practise.

Time: I reviewed my notes before I started writing. / I will contact you as soon as I receive the information.

5.7 Purpose

to, in order to, so that

  • She recorded herself to improve her pronunciation.
  • She recorded herself in order to identify her mistakes.
  • She recorded herself so that she could review her pronunciation.

6. Parallel Structure

Parallel structure means using the same grammatical form for related ideas.

Incorrect She enjoys reading, writing, and to speak English.
Correct She enjoys reading, writing, and speaking English.
Incorrect The course helps students improve their grammar, developing vocabulary, and to speak confidently.
Correct The course helps students improve their grammar, develop their vocabulary, and speak confidently.
Incorrect (Correlative) She is both intelligent and works hard.
Correct She is both intelligent and hardworking.
(Also: She both studies carefully and works hard.)

7. Varying Sentence Openings

Beginning every sentence in the same way makes writing repetitive. Sentence variety should make writing smoother, not more complicated.

Different Ways to Begin a Sentence

  • With the subject: The student completed the assignment.
  • With a time expression: After the lesson, the student completed the assignment.
  • With a dependent clause: Although she was tired, the student completed the assignment.
  • With an adverb: Fortunately, the student completed the assignment.
  • With a prepositional phrase: At the end of the lesson, the student asked a question.

8. Common Sentence Problems

8.1 Sentence Fragments

A fragment does not express a complete idea.

IncorrectBecause I wanted to improve my speaking.

CorrectI recorded myself because I wanted to improve my speaking.

IncorrectAlthough the lesson was difficult.

CorrectAlthough the lesson was difficult, I understood the main idea.

Incorrect (No Verb)The students in the classroom.

CorrectThe students are in the classroom.

8.2 Run-On Sentences

Joins complete ideas without correct punctuation or conjunctions.

Incorrect: I wanted to attend the lesson I was feeling unwell.

Correct: I wanted to attend the lesson, but I was feeling unwell.

Correct: Although I wanted to attend the lesson, I was feeling unwell.

8.3 Comma Splices

Two independent clauses joined only with a comma.

Incorrect: The lesson ended, the students left.

FANBOYS: The lesson ended, so the students left.

Semicolon: The lesson ended; the students left.

Two Sentences: The lesson ended. The students left.

8.4 Overloaded Sentences (Weak) I wanted to improve my English because I need it for work and I also want to travel and my friend recommended the course and the teacher seemed professional and I thought it would help me.
Stronger (Divided Logically) I wanted to improve my English because I need it for work and travel. A friend recommended the course, and the teacher appeared professional. For these reasons, I decided to join.
8.5 Too Many Short Sentences (Weak) Technology is useful. Students use it. Teachers use it. It saves time. It gives information.
Stronger Technology is useful for both students and teachers because it provides quick access to information and can save time during lessons.

9. Weak and Strong Sentence Comparisons

Weak

I was tired. I finished the work.

Stronger (Shows Contrast)

Although I was tired, I finished the work.

Weak

The internet provides information. Students should check the sources.

Stronger (Connects benefit/warning)

The internet provides easy access to information, but students should check whether their sources are reliable.

Weak

She practised every day. She improved.

Stronger (Cause & Result)

She practised every day, so she gradually improved.

Weak

I joined the course. I wanted to improve. The course was useful.

Stronger

I joined the course because I wanted to improve, and I found the lessons extremely useful.

10. Sentence Variety in a Paragraph

Weak Paragraph

"Online learning is flexible. Students study at home. Students save time. Students use technology. Online learning has disadvantages. Students may feel alone."

Problems:

  • Most sentences begin with "students".
  • Short and repetitive.
  • Relationships unclear.
  • Limited sentence variety.
Strong Paragraph

"Online learning offers students greater flexibility because they can study from home and organize lessons around their responsibilities. It can also save commuting time and provide easy access to digital materials. However, students may sometimes feel isolated if they have limited interaction with teachers and classmates. Therefore, online learning is most effective when flexibility is combined with regular communication and support."

Why It Is Stronger: Uses simple, compound, and complex sentences. Shows reason, addition, contrast, result. Varied openings.

Part Two: Speaking Skills

11. Organizing Ideas Before Speaking

Many learners know enough vocabulary but still struggle to speak clearly. The problem is often not vocabulary. It is organization. An organized answer usually contains: 1. Main idea, 2. Supporting points, 3. Explanation, 4. Example, 5. Conclusion.

Weak Organization

Q: What makes a good teacher?

"A good teacher is patient. Teachers explain. Students are different. Teachers should help. I had a good teacher before."

Problems: Ideas are not connected, example is incomplete, no clear development.

Strong Organization

"I believe patience is one of the most important qualities of a good teacher. Students learn at different speeds, so teachers need to explain ideas in more than one way. For example, one student may understand through a written explanation, while another may need a visual example. Therefore, a patient teacher can create a more supportive learning environment."

12. The Claim–Explanation–Example Technique

This technique helps you explain an idea clearly. Suitable for explaining a quality, skill, problem, benefit, disadvantage, or concept.

Claim

State the main idea.
A good teacher should be patient.

Explanation

Explain what it means or why it matters.
Students learn at different speeds, so teachers may need to explain the same idea in different ways.

Example

Give a specific example.
For example, some understand through rules, while others need visual examples.

13. ARE, PREP, and Claim–Explanation–Example

TechniqueBest useExample Topic
AREShort personal or preference answersQ: Do you prefer studying alone?
PREPOpinions and persuasive answersQ: Is time management important?
Claim–Explanation–ExampleExplaining qualities, concepts, or pointsQ: What makes someone reliable?

CEE Example in action:

"A reliable person keeps promises. This means that other people can trust them to do what they say. For example, if a reliable colleague agrees to complete a task by Friday, they make sure it is finished on time."

14. Using Keywords to Organize an Answer

Do not write complete sentences before every speaking task. Use keywords to organize the main ideas.

Q: Advantages of a large city?

  • - opportunities
  • - public transportation
  • - hospitals & universities
  • - entertainment
  • - traffic and noise
  • - conclusion

Developed Answer

"Living in a large city can provide many opportunities. People usually have better access to universities, hospitals, public transportation, and different types of employment. Large cities also offer more entertainment and social activities. However, traffic, noise, and high living costs can be serious disadvantages. Overall, city life may be suitable for people who value opportunity and convenience [conclusion]."

15. A Simple Spoken Answer Structure

For many general questions, use this 6-step flow:

1. Direct answer 2. First point 3. Explanation 4. Example 5. Second point 6. Conclusion

Q: What are the benefits of reading?

Reading has several important benefits. First, it helps people develop vocabulary and understand different writing styles. For example, language learners can notice how words are used naturally in context. Reading can also increase knowledge by introducing new ideas and experiences. Overall, regular reading can support both language development and personal growth.

16. Organizing More Than One Point

When your answer contains several points, signal the order clearly.

  • First of all...
  • The first point is...
  • Another important point is...
  • In addition...
  • On the other hand...
  • Finally... / Overall...

"There are two main reasons why people should learn a foreign language. First of all, it can improve professional opportunities. Many international companies prefer employees who can communicate with clients... Another important point is that language learning helps people understand other cultures. Overall, learning another language can support both career development and personal growth."

17. Asking Questions

Strong communication includes both answering and asking questions. Questions help you start conversations, show interest, clarify meaning, and understand opinions.

17.1 Yes-or-No Questions

Usually begin with: be, do, have, or modal verbs.

With Be: She is ready. → Is she ready?

With Have: Have you completed the assignment? / Has she arrived?

With Modals: Can you help me? / Should students use technology?

With Do/Does/Did: Does she speaks English?
Does she speak English? (Use the base verb)

17.2 WH-Questions

who, what, where, when, why, which, whose, how.

Question word + auxiliary + subject + main verb

"Why do you study English?"

  • Q-word: why | Aux: do | Subj: you | Verb: study
Subject Questions (who/what is the subject): An auxiliary is not normally used.
Correct: Who called you?
Incorrect: Who did call you? (Only for special emphasis)

17.3 Closed Questions

Produce short answers.

"Do you enjoy your job?" → "Yes, I do."

Open Questions

Encourage longer answers.

"What do you enjoy most?" → "I enjoy working with..."

18. Follow-Up Questions

Help continue a conversation.

  • - Why do you think that?
  • - Can you give an example?
  • - What happened next?
  • - How did that affect you?
  • - What do you mean by that?
  • - What are the advantages?
Developed Conversation: A: Do you enjoy teaching?
B: Yes, I do.
A: What do you enjoy most about it?
B: I enjoy seeing students become more confident.
A: Can you give an example?

19. Answering Directly

Begin by responding to the actual question.

Q: Do you prefer living in a city or a small town?

Indirect/Unclear: There are many cities in my country, and some people live in villages. Life is different everywhere.
Direct/Organized: I prefer living in a city because it offers more professional and educational opportunities. For example...

20. Common Speaking Organization Problems to Avoid:

  • Beginning without answering the question
  • Giving many unrelated details
  • Repeating the same point
  • Changing topics suddenly
  • Giving an example before explaining the point
  • Using too many memorized linking expressions
  • Speaking for a long time without a conclusion
  • Writing a complete script
  • Using difficult sentence structures you can't control
  • Stopping after every grammar mistake

21. Weak and Strong Spoken Answer Comparison

Question: What is an important life skill?
Weak Answer

"Communication is important. People communicate. It is useful at work and everywhere. I think it is important."

Problems: The same idea is repeated. The meaning of important is not explained. There is no clear example. The answer has no structure.
Strong Answer

"Communication is one of the most important life skills. It helps people express their needs clearly and understand others more effectively. For example, good communication can prevent misunderstandings between colleagues when they are working on the same project. Therefore, developing communication skills can improve both personal and professional relationships."

22 & 23. Self-Assessment Checklists

Speaking Self-Assessment

  • Did I answer the question directly?
  • Did I state one clear main idea?
  • Did I explain the idea and provide an example?
  • Did I organize multiple points clearly?
  • Did I use keywords rather than a full script?
  • Did I avoid unnecessary repetition?
  • Did I stay focused on the question?
  • Did I use an appropriate technique (ARE/PREP/CEE)?
  • Did I finish naturally & continue after a small mistake?
  • Did I sound spoken rather than memorized?

Writing Self-Editing

Clauses & Structure

  • Does every sentence contain a subject and verb?
  • Is every independent clause complete?
  • Is every dependent clause connected to an independent one?

Conjunctions & Problems

  • Comma before FANBOYS when joining complete clauses?
  • Did I punctuate however correctly?
  • Did I write any fragments or run-ons?
  • Did I avoid comma splices and overloaded sentences?

Variety

  • Did I combine related ideas and vary sentence openings?
  • Is every long sentence clear?
